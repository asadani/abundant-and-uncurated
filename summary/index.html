Abundant and Uncurated

Why synthetic data scales human judgement, and why it cannot become its source.

Machines should absorb the application of judgement, where they are demonstrably accurate and radically cheaper. Humans own two things that cannot be delegated: the formation of the criteria, and the supply of signal from outside the system.

01The usual defence of human curation fails

The common argument is that human judgement is simply better than machine judgement. It isn't, and the evidence is not close.

A language model beat crowd workers on annotation accuracy by roughly 25 percentage points across four datasets, at under $0.003 per annotation — about thirty times cheaper than the human baseline. Strong model judges agree with human preferences more than 80% of the time, which is the rate at which humans agree with each other.

Any argument for the human that rests on comparative accuracy has already lost. A better argument exists, and it is not about accuracy at all.

02Model collapse describes a regime, not a fact

The famous result — that training on model-generated data causes models to forget the true distribution, tails first — travelled stripped of its conditions. The outcome flips on a single variable. When each generation's real data is replaced by synthetic, test error rises every iteration. When synthetic data accumulates alongside the real data, collapse is avoided.

The replace paradigm drives the proportion of real data to zero after the first iteration — a condition describing no pipeline anyone operates.

Worth noting, because it cuts against a common heuristic: the peer-reviewed paper carries the less qualified claim; the preprints carry the more careful one. Weighting sources by venue gets this backwards.

03The mechanism that actually holds the line

Continual improvement requires curation that injects signal exogenous to the system that produced the data. Note what that specifies — exogenous, not human. At first this looks like it dissolves the case for people entirely: a verifier "whether a human or a better model" prevents collapse.

Read two sentences further and it inverts. Where a model supplies verification, gains plateau and may reverse unless that verifier is perfectly reliable, and retraining drives the estimate toward the verifier's own knowledge centre.

A system that learns only from itself is not learning. It is settling.

A model verifier does not add information. It transfers information the verifier already holds, and then the process terminates. The ceiling is informational, not engineering.

04Criteria cannot be pre-specified

It is impossible to completely determine evaluation criteria prior to human judging of model outputs. The circularity has a name — criteria drift: you need criteria to grade outputs, and grading outputs is how you discover the criteria. The obvious fix, grade first and specify after, was tested and fails.

This reconciles the disagreement. Every result favouring automation measures performance against a fixed rubric — and the rubric is the artefact that cannot be fixed in advance. It is a claim about logic, not capability. A better model makes the applying cheaper; it does not remove the step that comes first.

05Why curation stays expensive

  • Volume is continuous. One frontier alignment pipeline collected human preference data weekly, over 1 million binary comparisons. A standing operation, not a one-off dataset.
  • Failure is invisible until late. Data cascades are opaque and delayed, with poor indicators; 92% of surveyed practitioners hit at least one. Automated QA needs a quality signal to optimise against, and a process whose defects surface downstream does not emit one at the time of the work.
  • Curation is normative, not clerical. Annotation instructions reproduce and normalise the worldviews of requesters. That is why the cost has been displaced rather than removed — data workers are paid cents per task, typically without employment protections.

Synthetic data earns its place conditionally, and the binding condition is verification strength, not volume. Filtering helps only when verification gives a high-resolution correctness signal; naive filtering has produced no improvement at all, and over-strict filtering strips the diversity that correlates with downstream performance. Synthetic data inherits its quality from curated data. The curation didn't disappear. It moved upstream.

06The strongest case against this

One system trained by self-play with no human-curated data outperformed models trained on expert-curated human data in the coding category. It deserves to be taken seriously. Three bounds, none of which dismiss it:

The margin0.3 absolute percentage points, on one category. A near-tie the abstract framing obscures.
The scopeIt works because a code executor supplies free ground truth. That exists for code and mathematics; not for tone, safety or appropriateness.
The missing testA domain with no correctness oracle, where self-generated data improves a model across many generations with no human signal. Not yet run.

07The scarcity is partly a scarcity of permission

Two phenomena get blended. The forecast: training sets meet the stock of public human text between 2026 and 2032 — not yet observed. The measurement: over 28% of the most actively maintained critical sources in C4 are now fully restricted via robots.txt — already happened.

Exhaustion is a supply problem curation could address. Consent withdrawal is a governance problem it cannot touch: the data still exists and is simply no longer permitted.

Held at moderate confidence, and formally contested

What would falsify it: a domain with no formal correctness oracle in which self-generated data improves a model across many generations with no human signal at any point.

Two widely repeated claims failed verification and appear nowhere in the essay — that golden datasets from production traces beat synthetic test cases (the nearest source argues the opposite), and that a layered annotator/judge/gap-fill/human-review pipeline is now standard practice (no source establishes it).
